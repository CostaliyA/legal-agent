**Re: Whether *Moore v. United States* Supports a Refund Claim Involving Undistributed Foreign-Corporation Earnings**

Dear [Client]:

You asked whether *Moore v. United States*, 602 U.S. 572 (2024), supports a refund claim involving undistributed earnings of a foreign corporation. Based on the supplied excerpts, my preliminary view is that *Moore* does **not** support a broad claim that undistributed foreign-corporation earnings cannot be attributed to a U.S. shareholder. It is affirmatively adverse to that core theory. *Moore* may, however, preserve narrower claims if the facts show arbitrary attribution, double taxation of the same undistributed income, no entity-level realized income, or a statutory or procedural defect. We need the facts listed below before recommending a filing.

**I. The Tax and Procedural Record in *Moore***

The MRT was a one-time, backward-looking tax enacted as part of the 2017 tax legislation. It attributed the long-accumulated and undistributed income of American-controlled foreign corporations to American shareholders and taxed those shareholders on their pro rata shares at rates from 8 to 15.5 percent. *Moore* op. at 580 (citing §§ 965(a), (c), (d)). Before the 2017 Act, the foreign corporations themselves were generally not taxed on that income, and, apart from subpart F, the undistributed income of those foreign corporations was not attributed to American shareholders. *Id.* at 579–580.

The Moores invested $40,000 in 2006 in KisanKraft, an American-controlled foreign corporation in India, and received a 13-percent ownership share. KisanKraft generated substantial income but, as of 2017, had not distributed it to its American shareholders. Neither KisanKraft nor the Moores had paid U.S. taxes on that income. *Id.* at 580. By the end of 2017, the Moores’ pro rata share of KisanKraft’s accumulated income from 2006 to 2017 was about $508,000. After a deduction not relevant here, the Moores declared $132,512 in MRT income and owed $14,729. They paid and sued for a refund. *Id.*

The Moores argued that the MRT violated the Direct Tax Clause because it was an unapportioned direct tax on their shares, and that it violated the Due Process Clause because it applied retroactively. The District Court dismissed, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed. *Id.* at 580–581. The Supreme Court granted certiorari only on the Direct Tax Clause argument. *Id.* at 581. The Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit. *Id.* at 600.

**II. The Attribution Holding**

The majority framed the “precise and narrow question” as whether Congress may attribute an entity’s realized and undistributed income to the entity’s shareholders or partners and then tax those shareholders or partners on their portions of that income. The Court answered yes. *Id.* at 584. The Court reasoned that the MRT taxes income realized by KisanKraft, attributes that income to the shareholders, and taxes the shareholders on their share of the undistributed corporate income. *Id.*

The Court relied on longstanding precedents and congressional practice allowing Congress either to tax the entity on its income or to attribute the entity’s undistributed income to shareholders or partners and tax them. *Id.* at 585–592 (discussing *Burk-Waggoner Oil Assn. v. Hopkins*, *Heiner v. Mellon*, *Helvering v. National Grocery Co.*, partnerships, S corporations, and subpart F). The Court also rejected the Moores’ reliance on *Eisner v. Macomber* for the attribution issue. According to the majority, *Macomber* was not a case about Congress’s power to attribute entity income to shareholders or partners, and whatever else it might stand for, it does not proscribe attribution. *Id.* at 587–589.

The Court also rejected the Moores’ attempted distinctions. It held that the Moores’ “constructive realization” label was not used in the case law or the Internal Revenue Code and did not distinguish the MRT from subpart F and other pass-through taxes. *Id.* at 594–596. Because the MRT is integrated into subpart F’s framework and has the same essential features, the Court concluded that the MRT likewise taxes so-called constructively realized income. *Id.* at 595–596.

The majority emphasized that its holding is narrow. It is limited to:

1. taxation of the shareholders of an entity;
2. on the undistributed income realized by the entity;
3. which has been attributed to the shareholders;
4. when the entity itself has not been taxed on that income.

In other words, the holding applies when Congress treats the entity as a pass-through. *Id.* at 598.

**III. Constitutional Limits Left Open or Preserved**

*Moore* does not decide whether realization is a constitutional requirement for an income tax. The majority expressly stated: “We do not decide that question today.” *Id.* at 598. It noted that the dissent and the concurrence focus primarily on realization, and that the Government and the Moores disagree on whether realization is constitutionally required. The Court said it need not resolve that disagreement to decide *Moore*. *Id.* at 598–599.

*Moore* does preserve some limits. The majority stated that the Due Process Clause proscribes arbitrary attribution, and that limits on attribution may be based on the taxpayer’s relationship to the underlying income. *Id.* at 590 n.4; *id.* at 599. The Court also stated that nothing in its opinion should be read to authorize a hypothetical congressional effort to tax both an entity and its shareholders or partners on the same undistributed income realized by the entity; in such a scenario, the entity would not simply be a traditional pass-through. *Id.* at 599. The Court further noted that other kinds of taxes could raise different issues, including a hypothetical unapportioned tax on wealth or net worth, or a tax on appreciation, the constitutionality of which may depend on whether realization is required. The Court did not resolve those issues. *Id.* at 599.

The Court also did not adopt the concurrence’s suggested line between closely held foreign corporations and widely held or domestic corporations. The majority expressly disagreed that its precedents draw such a line and stated that its opinion does not draw such a line. *Id.* at 598 n.8. Justice Barrett’s concurrence suggested that a different tax—for example, a tax on shareholders of a widely held or domestic corporation—would present a different case. *Id.* at 604 (Barrett, J., concurring in judgment). That view is not the majority holding.

**IV. The Competing Opinions**

Justice Jackson concurred and joined the Court’s opinion in full. He emphasized that Congress has plenary tax power and that the realization requirement appears nowhere in the text of the Sixteenth Amendment. He also argued that *Macomber*’s realization requirement has been limited or undermined by later cases and that there is no constitutional requirement that a taxpayer be able to sever gain from original capital before being taxed. *Id.* at 600–603 (Jackson, J., concurring).

Justice Barrett, joined by Justice Alito, concurred in the judgment but disagreed with the majority’s reasoning on attribution. She concluded that the Moores had not realized income from their KisanKraft shares because they received no dividend and did not sell or otherwise dispose of their shares. *Id.* at 604–607 (Barrett, J., concurring in judgment). She argued that the Sixteenth Amendment’s reference to income “derived” from a source encompasses a realization requirement. *Id.* at 606. She also argued that Congress’s power to attribute corporate income to shareholders is limited and depends on the relationship between the shareholder and the income. *Id.* at 617–618. She described the Court’s holding as narrow and noted that a different tax, such as one on shareholders of a widely held or domestic corporation, would present a different case. *Id.* at 604, 618.

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Gorsuch, dissented. He stated that the Moores paid $14,729 in taxes on an investment that never yielded them a penny. He would have held that realization is a constitutional requirement for income taxes and that the majority’s attribution doctrine is an unsupported invention. *Id.* at 620 (Thomas, J., dissenting). He also criticized the majority for not addressing the question presented—whether Congress may tax unrealized sums without apportionment. *Id.* at 620–621.

**V. Application to a Refund Claim Involving Undistributed Foreign-Corporation Earnings**

Under the hierarchy of authority, the final majority opinion controls over the petitioner’s brief and the separate opinions. The petitioner’s brief argued that realization is required and that the MRT is a tax on property, not income. *See* moore-petitioner-brief.pdf, p. 17–18. That is advocacy, not a final holding. The Supreme Court rejected the property-tax characterization and did not adopt the brief’s broad realization rule; it expressly left the realization question open. *Compare* moore-petitioner-brief.pdf, p. 17–18 *with* *Moore* op. at 584, 598–599. The final 2024 decision controls over the pre-decision brief.

Applying *Moore*, a refund claim based on the theory that undistributed foreign-corporation earnings are not the shareholder’s income until distributed is likely to fail. The majority held that Congress may attribute an entity’s realized and undistributed income to shareholders and tax them on that income when the entity itself has not been taxed and Congress treats the entity as a pass-through. *Id.* at 584, 598. A claim that the MRT is an unapportioned direct tax on property was rejected. *Id.* at 584, 598. A claim that subpart F and the MRT are distinguishable because subpart F involves “constructive realization” was also rejected. *Id.* at 594–596.

A refund claim may be viable only if it fits outside *Moore*’s narrow holding. Potential openings include:

1. **Arbitrary attribution.** If the client’s relationship to the underlying income is too attenuated, or if the attribution is arbitrary under the Due Process Clause, *Moore* preserves that challenge. *Id.* at 590 n.4, 599.

2. **Double taxation of the same undistributed income.** If the entity itself was taxed on the same undistributed income that was attributed to the client, *Moore*’s narrow holding may not apply, and the Court stated that nothing in its opinion authorizes taxing both the entity and the shareholder on the same undistributed income. *Id.* at 598–599.

3. **No entity-level realized income.** *Moore*’s holding assumes that the entity realized the undistributed income that is attributed. If the item is merely unrealized appreciation or a tax on property value, *Moore* does not resolve that issue. *Id.* at 598–599.

4. **Retroactivity.** The Moores raised a due process retroactivity argument in the lower courts, which the Ninth Circuit rejected. *Id.* at 581, 597 n.6. The Supreme Court did not decide that issue because the Moores raised only the Direct Tax Clause. *Id.* at 581. A retroactivity-based claim would require separate analysis and facts about the tax years and the client’s reliance.

5. **Statutory or procedural defects.** *Moore* is a constitutional decision. It does not establish that every MRT or subpart F assessment was correctly computed or that statutory eligibility, ownership thresholds, basis, foreign tax credit, treaty, or refund-procedure requirements were satisfied. *Id.* at 584, 598.

6. **Widely held or domestic corporation.** Justice Barrett’s concurrence suggested that a tax on shareholders of a widely held or domestic corporation could present a different case. *Id.* at 604 (Barrett, J., concurring in judgment). But the majority expressly declined to draw that line. *Id.* at 598 n.8. This is an argument, not a holding.

**VI. Facts Counsel Needs Before Recommending a Filing**

Before we can recommend a refund claim, we need to confirm the following:

- The identity, country, and status of the foreign corporation, including whether it is an American-controlled foreign corporation or a controlled foreign corporation, and the client’s ownership percentage and class of stock.
- The exact Code provision under which the tax was assessed—for example, § 965, subpart F, § 951A, or another provision—and the tax year or years at issue.
- Whether the foreign corporation realized the income that was attributed, the amount and character of that income, and how it was computed.
- Whether the foreign corporation itself paid U.S. tax on the same income, and whether there is any double taxation of the same undistributed income.
- Whether the corporation made any distributions, and whether the client sold or otherwise disposed of shares.
- The client’s relationship to the underlying income, control rights, and any facts suggesting that the attribution is arbitrary.
- The date the tax was paid, the date any refund claim was filed, and all procedural documents, including returns, IRS notices, assessments, waivers, closing agreements, and prior litigation.
- Whether the claim challenges the constitutionality of the statute, the calculation of the tax, the ownership or control thresholds, or a procedural requirement.
- The client’s objectives, the amount in controversy, litigation costs, and risk tolerance.

**VII. Conclusion**

Based on the supplied excerpts, *Moore* does not provide a broad basis for a refund claim involving undistributed foreign-corporation earnings. It is more likely to defeat a claim premised on the argument that undistributed foreign earnings cannot be attributed to U.S. shareholders without a distribution. A filing may be warranted only if the facts place the claim outside *Moore*’s narrow holding—for example, arbitrary attribution, double taxation of the same undistributed income, no entity-level realized income, or a statutory or procedural defect.

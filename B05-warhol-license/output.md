## 1. Executive Clearance Position

1. **The 1984 Vanity Fair license was narrow and one-time.** Goldsmith licensed her 1981 Prince photograph to Vanity Fair as an “artist reference for an illustration” for the November 1984 issue, with the express terms that the illustration could appear “one time full page and one time under one quarter page” and “[n]o other usage right granted.” Goldsmith was to receive $400 and a source credit. `warhol-goldsmith-supreme-court-opinion.pdf`, slip op. at 3. The Supreme Court described the condition as “one time” only. *Id.* at 1–2.

2. **The 1984 license did not authorize the Prince Series or later commercial licensing.** The Court states that, “[i]n addition to the single illustration authorized by the Vanity Fair license,” Warhol created 15 other works based on Goldsmith’s photograph—13 silkscreen prints and two pencil drawings. *Id.* at 4–5. Those additional works are the “Prince Series.” *Id.* at 5.

3. **The 2016 Condé Nast transaction is a separate commercial license, not a continuation of the 1984 Vanity Fair license.** After Prince died in 2016, Condé Nast contacted AWF about reusing the 1984 Vanity Fair image for a special commemorative edition; after AWF told Condé Nast about the other Prince Series images, Condé Nast licensed **Orange Prince** instead. The magazine was titled “The Genius of Prince,” was “devoted to Prince,” and Condé Nast paid AWF $10,000. Goldsmith received neither a fee nor a source credit. *Id.* at 5–6.

4. **The Supreme Court held that the first fair-use factor favors Goldsmith as to the 2016 Condé Nast license.** The specific challenged use was AWF’s commercial licensing of Orange Prince to Condé Nast. The Court held that the original photograph and the copying use “share substantially the same purpose,” the use is commercial, and AWF offered no persuasive independent justification. The first factor therefore favors Goldsmith. *Id.* at 13, 24–25, 38. Because AWF did not challenge the Second Circuit’s determinations that the second, third, and fourth factors also favor Goldsmith, the fair-use defense fails for that use. *Id.* at 38.

5. **The decision is use-specific, not a blanket rule that all Warhol Prince works, or all future uses, are unfair.** The Court expressly limited its analysis to the 2016 Condé Nast licensing and expressed “no opinion as to the creation, display, or sale of any of the original Prince Series works.” *Id.* at 21. It also stated that “not all of Warhol’s works, nor all uses of them, give rise to the same fair use analysis.” *Id.* at 27. Future uses require separate clearance analysis.

6. **Clearance recommendation:** Treat any proposed commercial licensing of a Prince Series image to a magazine, commemorative edition, or similar editorial product about Prince—especially a cover or feature—as **high risk** and not cleared by fair use on the 2016 record. Do not rely on the 1984 Vanity Fair license for any post-1984 use. Do not rely on AWF’s asserted copyright in the Prince Series as a substitute for a license from Goldsmith or her successor. Obtain a written Goldsmith license or a written fair-use opinion specific to the proposed use. Museum, scholarly, or art-commentary uses may present stronger fair-use arguments under the Court’s reasoning, but they are not categorically cleared.

## 3. The Goldsmith License Chain

### 3.1 Goldsmith’s original photograph and copyright

Goldsmith photographed Prince in 1981 for *Newsweek*, including a black-and-white studio portrait. *Newsweek* ran a different concert photograph; Goldsmith retained the other photographs and “holds copyright in all of them.” SCOTUS slip op. at 3. The black-and-white studio portrait is the original copyrighted work at issue. *Id.*

### 3.2 The 1984 Vanity Fair license

In 1984, Goldsmith, through her agency, licensed that photograph to Vanity Fair “to serve as an ‘artist reference for an illustration’ in the magazine.” *Id.* The license terms were:

> “to be published in Vanity Fair November 1984 issue. It can appear one time full page and one time under one quarter page. No other usage right granted.” *Id.*

Goldsmith was to receive $400 and a source credit. *Id.* The Court elsewhere describes the condition as “for ‘one time’ only.” *Id.* at 1–2.

Vanity Fair hired Warhol to make the illustration. Warhol made a silkscreen using Goldsmith’s photograph; Vanity Fair published the resulting image alongside the November 1984 article “Purple Fame,” credited Goldsmith for the “source photograph,” and paid her $400. *Id.* at 2, 4.

**Clearance point:** The 1984 license was an executed, one-time license for a single Vanity Fair illustration in the November 1984 issue. It was not a license for the Prince Series, for later magazine covers, for commercial merchandising, or for future derivative licensing.

### 3.3 Warhol’s additional Prince Series works

“In addition to the single illustration authorized by the Vanity Fair license,” Warhol created 15 other works based on Goldsmith’s photograph: 13 silkscreen prints and two pencil drawings. *Id.* at 4–5. These are collectively the “Prince Series.” *Id.* at 5. Goldsmith did not know about the Prince Series until 2016, when she saw Orange Prince on the cover of a Condé Nast magazine. *Id.*

By 2016, Warhol had died, and the Prince Series had passed to AWF. AWF no longer possesses the works—it sold 12 and transferred custody of the remaining four to the Andy Warhol Museum—but it asserts copyright in them and has licensed images for commercial and editorial uses. *Id.* at 5 & n.2.

**Clearance point:** AWF’s asserted copyright in the Prince Series is distinct from Goldsmith’s copyright in the underlying photograph. A derivative work can itself infringe the underlying work; AWF’s ownership of the Prince Series does not clear Goldsmith’s rights. The Supreme Court did not decide the separate infringement merits. *See id.* at 50 (Gorsuch, J., concurring) (Court “does not even decide whether the Foundation’s image of Prince infringes”).

### 3.4 The 2016 Condé Nast license

After Prince died in 2016, Condé Nast contacted AWF about reusing the 1984 Vanity Fair image for a special commemorative edition. After AWF informed Condé Nast about the other Prince Series images, Condé Nast obtained a license to publish Orange Prince instead. The magazine, “The Genius of Prince,” was a tribute “devoted to Prince.” Condé Nast paid AWF $10,000. Goldsmith received neither a fee nor a source credit. *Id.* at 5–6. The magazine contained numerous concert and studio photographs of Prince, and Orange Prince appeared on the cover. *Id.* at 22.

**Clearance point:** The 2016 Condé Nast license is the specific transaction the Supreme Court addressed. It is separate from the 1984 Vanity Fair license and separate from any proposed future use.

## 4. The Supreme Court’s Use-Specific Fair-Use Reasoning

### 4.1 The holding

The Supreme Court framed the sole question as whether the first fair-use factor—“the purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes,” 17 U.S.C. § 107(1)—weighs in favor of AWF’s “recent commercial licensing to Condé Nast.” SCOTUS slip op. at 2. The Court agreed with the Second Circuit that the first factor favors Goldsmith, not AWF. *Id.* at 2, 13.

The Court explained that the specific use alleged to infringe was AWF’s licensing of Orange Prince to Condé Nast. As portraits of Prince used to depict Prince in magazine stories about Prince, the original photograph and the copying use “share substantially the same purpose.” The use was also commercial. Even though Orange Prince adds new expression, the first factor still favors Goldsmith in the context of the challenged use. *Id.* at 12–13.

The Court concluded:

> “Goldsmith’s original photograph of Prince, and AWF’s copying use of that photograph in an image licensed to a special edition magazine devoted to Prince, share substantially the same purpose, and the use is of a commercial nature. AWF has offered no other persuasive justification for its unauthorized use of the photograph. Therefore, the ‘purpose and character of the use’ . . . weighs in Goldsmith’s favor.” *Id.* at 38.

AWF did not challenge the Second Circuit’s determinations that the second, third, and fourth factors favor Goldsmith. Because the first factor also favors her, the judgment was affirmed. *Id.* at 38.

### 4.2 Why the use was not transformative in the relevant sense

The Court rejected AWF’s argument that new meaning or message alone makes a use transformative. The first factor asks whether the allegedly infringing use has a “further purpose or different character,” and the degree of difference must be weighed against commercialism. New expression may be relevant, but it is “not, without more, dispositive of the first factor.” *Id.* at 12.

The Court emphasized that “transformative” is a matter of degree and must be understood against the copyright owner’s exclusive right to prepare derivative works. An overbroad concept of transformative use “would narrow the copyright owner’s exclusive right to create derivative works.” *Id.* at 17. The degree of transformation required for fair use “must go beyond that required to qualify as a derivative.” *Id.*

The Court distinguished Warhol’s Campbell’s Soup Cans works. The Soup Cans series used Campbell’s logo for artistic commentary on consumerism, a purpose “orthogonal to advertising soup,” and targeted the logo itself. *Id.* at 27–28. By contrast, AWF’s use of Goldsmith’s photograph did not target the photograph, and AWF offered no other compelling justification. *Id.* at 28, 34–35. The Court noted that AWF never maintained that the Prince Series works commented on, criticized, or otherwise targeted Goldsmith’s photograph. *Id.* at 34 n.20.

### 4.3 The commercial nature and same-purpose finding

The Court found that AWF’s use was commercial: Goldsmith licensed her photograph to Vanity Fair for $400; AWF licensed Orange Prince to Condé Nast for $10,000. The commercial character, “though not dispositive,” tends to weigh against fair use. *Id.* at 24. Taken together—same substantially purpose plus commercial nature—the two elements “counsel against fair use, absent some other justification for copying.” *Id.* at 25.

The Court cautioned that it did not define the purpose as simply “commercial” or “commercial licensing.” It found significant the “degree of similarity between the specific purposes of the original work and the secondary use at issue.” *Id.* at 23 n.11. It also noted that the analysis “might be different if Orange Prince appeared in an art magazine alongside an article about Warhol.” *Id.* at 24 n.12.

### 4.4 Scope limitation

The Court expressly limited its analysis to the 2016 Condé Nast licensing:

> “Only that last use, however, AWF’s commercial licensing of Orange Prince to Condé Nast, is alleged to be infringing. We limit our analysis accordingly. In particular, the Court expresses no opinion as to the creation, display, or sale of any of the original Prince Series works.” *Id.* at 21.

Goldsmith had abandoned all claims except the 2016 Condé Nast license and prospective relief as to similar commercial licensing. *Id.* at 21 n.9. The decision is therefore not a blanket adjudication of the Prince Series or of every future use.

## 5. Competing Arguments and Their Treatment

### 5.1 AWF’s argument: new meaning or message makes the use transformative

AWF’s petitioner brief argued that a follow-on work is transformative when it conveys a different meaning or message from its source, and that the Second Circuit erred by refusing to consider the work’s meaning. `warhol-petitioner-brief.pdf`, at 2, 16–17. AWF relied on *Campbell*’s language about “new expression, meaning, or message.” *Id.* at 23.

The Supreme Court rejected that as a sufficient test. It held that new expression may be relevant to whether the use has a distinct purpose or character, but it is not dispositive. SCOTUS slip op. at 12. The Court stated that *Campbell* “cannot be read to mean that § 107(1) weighs in favor of any use that adds some new expression, meaning, or message,” because that would let transformative use swallow the derivative-work right. *Id.* at 28. Meaning or message is relevant only to whether the new use serves a purpose distinct from the original, or instead supersedes its objects. *Id.* at 29–30.

### 5.2 Goldsmith’s and the Second Circuit’s argument: same purpose and market substitution

The Second Circuit found that the “purpose and function of the two works at issue here is identical, not merely in the broad sense that they are created as works of visual art, but also in the narrow but essential sense that they are portraits of the same person.” *Id.* at 10. It also found that AWF’s commercial licensing encroached on Goldsmith’s protected market to license her photograph “to publications for editorial purposes and to other artists to create derivative works.” *Id.* at 11.

The Supreme Court agreed with the same-purpose and commercial analysis for the 2016 use. It found that both Goldsmith and AWF sold images of Prince—AWF’s copying Goldsmith’s—to magazines to illustrate stories about the celebrity, which is the typical use of Goldsmith’s photographs. *Id.* at 13, 24–25. It rejected the idea that aesthetic variations alone changed the essential purpose of depicting Prince in a commemorative magazine. *Id.* at 23 n.11.

### 5.3 The concurrence: focus on the challenged use, not artist intent

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justice Jackson, concurred. He agreed that the first factor requires courts to assess the purpose and character of the particular challenged use, not to speculate about the artist’s intent or to act as art critics. *Id.* at 46–48 (Gorsuch, J., concurring). He also noted that the Court did not decide whether the Foundation’s image infringes Goldsmith’s copyright. *Id.* at 50. And he observed that if AWF had sought to display Warhol’s image in a nonprofit museum or a for-profit book commenting on 20th-century art, “the purpose and character of that use might well point to fair use.” *Id.* at 51.

The concurrence is not the majority’s reasoning, but it reinforces that the decision is use-specific and that future museum, scholarly, or art-commentary uses may require separate analysis.

### 5.4 The dissent: broader transformativeness view, not controlling

Justice Kagan, joined by Chief Justice Roberts, dissented. The dissent described Warhol as “the avatar of transformative copying” and would give greater weight to the aesthetic and expressive differences between the silkscreen and the photograph. *Id.* at 52–55. It criticized the majority for allowing commercial licensing to defeat creativity. *Id.* at 53–54.

The dissent is not controlling. For clearance purposes, the majority’s use-specific same-purpose/commercial analysis governs the 2016 Condé Nast license. The dissent may inform a future fair-use argument, but it cannot be relied on as the law.

## 6. Separating the 2016 Magazine License from Future Uses

### 6.1 The 2016 license is not cleared by fair use

For the 2016 Condé Nast license, the Supreme Court held that the first fair-use factor favors Goldsmith; AWF did not challenge the other factors; and the fair-use defense therefore fails for that use. SCOTUS slip op. at 38. The Court did not decide the separate infringement merits. *Id.* at 50 (Gorsuch, J., concurring). For clearance purposes, the 2016 transaction should be treated as **not fair use** and not as a safe template for similar commercial magazine licensing.

### 6.2 The 1984 Vanity Fair license does not cover future uses

The 1984 license was limited to one illustration in the November 1984 Vanity Fair issue, with “one time full page and one time under one quarter page” and “[n]o other usage right granted.” *Id.* at 3. It does not authorize:

- the Prince Series;
- the 2016 Condé Nast special edition;
- future magazine covers or commemorative editions;
- merchandise, advertising, or social-media uses;
- sublicensing or derivative licensing by AWF.

Any reliance on the 1984 license for post-1984 uses would contradict its express terms.

### 6.3 Future uses require use-by-use analysis

The Supreme Court’s decision is not a blanket rule that all Prince Series uses are unfair. The Court said “not all of Warhol’s works, nor all uses of them, give rise to the same fair use analysis.” *Id.* at 27. It suggested that an art magazine article about Warhol might produce a different first-factor analysis. *Id.* at 24 n.12. The concurrence suggested that nonprofit museum display or a for-profit art-commentary book “might well point to fair use.” *Id.* at 51.

But those statements are not holdings. For future licensing, the Foundation should analyze:

- **Specific use:** Is the image used on a magazine cover or in a commemorative edition about Prince, or in an art-critical, scholarly, or museum context?
- **Purpose and character:** Does the use serve a purpose distinct from Goldsmith’s typical licensing of Prince portraits to magazines? Does it comment on, criticize, or target Goldsmith’s photograph, or is it merely a new aesthetic version of the same subject?
- **Commerciality:** Is the use commercial, paid, advertising-related, or promotional?
- **Substitution:** Does the use substitute for Goldsmith’s photograph or her derivative/editorial licensing market?
- **Justification:** Is there an independent justification for copying the photograph, beyond “we can make it better” or “it has new meaning”? *See id.* at 34 n.21.
- **Scope of any license:** Does the 2016 Condé Nast license, or any other executed license, actually authorize the proposed use? The terms of the 2016 license are not supplied.

### 6.4 Practical risk categories

| Proposed use | Clearance risk | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Licensing Orange Prince or another Prince Series image to a magazine or commemorative edition about Prince, especially a cover | **High / do not rely on fair use** | Directly analogous to the 2016 Condé Nast use; same purpose, commercial; Supreme Court held first factor favors Goldsmith. |
| Commercial merchandise, advertising, or promotional use of Prince Series images | **High** | Commercial use plus likely substitution for Goldsmith’s derivative/editorial markets; no independent justification shown. |
| Museum exhibition, nonprofit display, or art-commentary book about Warhol/20th-century art | **Uncertain / possible fair use** | Concurrence and majority dicta suggest different analysis may apply, but no holding; facts must show distinct purpose and non-substitution. |
| Scholarly or critical use that targets or comments on Goldsmith’s photograph itself | **Potentially stronger** | The Court emphasized targeting and independent justification; but this was not the 2016 use and must be documented. |
| Any use relying on the 1984 Vanity Fair license | **Not cleared** | License was one-time, November 1984 issue, no other usage rights. |
| Any use relying solely on AWF’s copyright in the Prince Series | **Not cleared** | AWF’s derivative copyright does not resolve Goldsmith’s underlying copyright. |

## 7. Unresolved Facts and Documents to Obtain

The following remain unresolved on the supplied excerpts:

1. **Complete 1984 Vanity Fair license.** We have the Court’s quotation and description, but not the full instrument. We should confirm the parties, term, territory, media, sublicensing, derivative-rights, credit, and indemnity provisions.
2. **Complete 2016 AWF–Condé Nast license.** The opinion describes the transaction and the $10,000 payment, but not the license’s term, territory, media, exclusivity, sublicensing, derivative-rights, credit, or indemnity provisions.
3. **Any other Goldsmith–Vanity Fair/AWF agreements.** The excerpts do not show whether any later license, release, or settlement exists.
4. **Current copyright ownership and registration status.** The opinion states Goldsmith holds copyright in her photographs and AWF asserts copyright in the Prince Series, but the excerpts do not include registration, renewal, or chain-of-title documents.
5. **Infringement merits.** The Supreme Court did not decide whether the Prince Series infringes Goldsmith’s copyright; the concurrence expressly notes that it did not. *Id.* at 50 (Gorsuch, J., concurring). The substantial-similarity ruling was not challenged in the Supreme Court. *Id.* at 2, 12.
6. **Whether a proposed future use targets or comments on Goldsmith’s photograph.** The record shows AWF did not make that claim for the 2016 use. *Id.* at 34 n.20. Future clearance depends on facts not in the excerpts.
7. **Whether the 2016 Condé Nast license remains in effect or was limited to a single edition.** The excerpts do not state its duration or scope beyond the 2016 special edition.
8. **Whether any future use is commercial, editorial, museum, scholarly, or promotional.** This must be documented for each proposed license.

## 8. Recommended Clearance Protocol

1. **Do not treat the 2016 Condé Nast transaction as a fair-use precedent for future commercial magazine licensing.** It is the specific use the Supreme Court held weighed against fair use.
2. **Do not rely on the 1984 Vanity Fair license for any post-1984 use.** Its text is expressly limited to the November 1984 issue and grants no other usage rights.
3. **Do not rely on AWF’s Prince Series copyright alone.** Obtain a written license from Goldsmith or her successor for any use that implicates her photograph, or obtain a written fair-use opinion specific to the proposed use.
4. **For magazine, commemorative, cover, merchandise, advertising, or promotional uses, require Goldsmith clearance.** These are closest to the 2016 holding and carry the highest risk.
5. **For museum, scholarly, or art-commentary uses, document the distinct purpose.** Identify whether the use comments on, criticizes, or targets Goldsmith’s photograph; whether it is commercial; and whether it substitutes for Goldsmith’s licensing markets. Do not assume fair use.
6. **Review the actual 2016 and 1984 instruments before licensing.** The excerpts describe them but do not supply full terms.
7. **Escalate any proposed use that is similar to the 2016 Condé Nast cover for a written fair-use analysis.** The Supreme Court’s same-purpose and commercial findings make that category particularly risky.

---

**Bottom line:** The 1984 Vanity Fair license was a narrow one-time license. The 2016 Condé Nast license was a separate commercial transaction, and the Supreme Court held that the first fair-use factor favors Goldsmith for that specific use. Future uses are not categorically resolved; they must be cleared use-by-use, with particular caution for commercial magazine or commemorative licensing of Prince Series images.

**PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL — ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT**

**Creditor Update — Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.**  
**Re:** Supreme Court decision on the proposed Sackler third-party release; statutory holding; open issues; revised-plan choices

This update is based on the supplied excerpts from the Supreme Court opinion and Purdue’s respondent brief. It does not address later docket developments or the full plan/confirmation record. Citations are to the supplied page-proof opinion (“Op.”) and respondent brief (“Resp. Br.”).

---

## I. Bottom line

The Supreme Court reversed the Second Circuit and held that the Bankruptcy Code **does not authorize** a release and injunction that, as part of a Chapter 11 plan, “effectively seeks to discharge claims against a nondebtor without the consent of affected claimants.” Op. 226–227. The Court did **not** hold that all third-party releases are unlawful. It expressly left consensual third-party releases undisturbed and left several related questions open. Op. 226–227.

For Purdue creditors, the practical consequence is that the proposed Sackler release cannot be imposed on non-consenting opioid claimants through the plan. A revised plan must either obtain legally sufficient consent from affected claimants, omit the nonconsensual third-party release, or pursue another structure that the Court left open. The prior plan was stayed, not an executed and effective instrument. Op. 226.

---

## II. What was proposed

The proposed “Sackler discharge” comprised a release and an injunction. As described by the Court, it would have:

- voided current and future opioid-related claims against the Sackler family and related entities;
- barred claims by creditors participating in the bankruptcy and by anyone who might otherwise sue Purdue;
- extinguished not only negligence claims but also claims for fraud and willful misconduct; and
- enjoined claims “forever,” running in favor of hundreds, if not thousands, of Sackler family members and entities. Op. 211–212.

The Sacklers initially proposed to return $4.325 billion of the approximately $11 billion they had withdrawn from Purdue between 2008 and 2016, payable over a decade, in exchange for estate claims being extinguished and the opioid litigation against them being ended. Op. 211. During the Second Circuit appeal, plan proponents floated an additional $1.175 to $1.675 billion contribution if eight objecting States and the District of Columbia withdrew their objections. Op. 213. Plan proponents described the total proposed contribution as $5.5 to $6 billion. Resp. Br. 11. That contribution was a proposal, not an executed commitment in the excerpts.

The plan also proposed to reorganize Purdue as a public benefit company dedicated primarily to opioid education and abatement, and to pay individual victims from a base amount of $3,500 up to $48,000 before deductions, with larger payments in installments over as many as ten years. Op. 212. Creditors were polled; fewer than 20% of eligible creditors participated, though most who returned ballots supported the plan. Thousands of opioid victims voted against it. Op. 212.

Procedurally, the bankruptcy court confirmed the plan; the district court vacated confirmation; the Second Circuit reversed and revived the confirmation order; the Supreme Court granted a stay and then reversed the Second Circuit. Op. 213–214, 226–227.

---

## III. The statutory holding

The Court’s holding is narrow but firm: the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize a nonconsensual third-party release and injunction of the kind proposed here.

The Court focused on **11 U.S.C. § 1123(b)(6)**, the catchall permitting “any other appropriate provision not inconsistent with the applicable provisions of this title.” Op. 215–216. The Court reasoned that:

- Paragraphs (1) through (5) of § 1123(b) concern the debtor, its estate, and the debtor-creditor relationship. Op. 216–219.
- A catchall like paragraph (6) must be read in context and generally “embrace only objects similar in nature” to the specific preceding examples. Op. 217–219.
- Section 1123(b)(6) could not be read to give bankruptcy courts the “radically different” power to discharge nondebtor debts without the consent of affected claimants. Op. 218–221.
- Section 105(a) does not independently authorize the release; it only carries out authorities conferred elsewhere, and Purdue conceded that point. Op. 217 n.2.
- Section 524(e) provides that a debtor’s discharge generally does not affect the liability of another entity. Op. 215, 220.
- Section 524(g), which permits certain third-party injunctions in asbestos cases, is a notable exception that makes it less likely § 1123(b)(6) grants that power in every context. Op. 220–221.
- The proposed release would exceed ordinary discharge limits by extinguishing fraud, willful-injury, and wrongful-death claims without consent and without the Sacklers placing anything approaching their full assets on the table. Op. 222–223.
- Calling the relief a “release” rather than a “discharge” did not change the analysis. Op. 223.

The Court therefore reversed the Second Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings. Op. 226–227.

---

## IV. What the Court left open

The Court expressly identified several issues it did **not** decide:

1. **Consensual third-party releases.** The decision “should [not] be construed to call into question consensual third-party releases offered in connection with a bankruptcy reorganization plan.” Those pose different questions and may rest on different legal grounds. Op. 226.
2. **What counts as “consensual.”** The Court did not define what qualifies as a consensual release. Op. 226.
3. **Full-satisfaction plans.** The Court did not pass on a plan that provides for the full satisfaction of claims against a third-party nondebtor. Op. 226.
4. **Unwinding effective plans.** Because this case involved only a stayed plan, the Court did not address whether its reading of the Code would justify unwinding plans that had already become effective and been substantially consummated. Op. 226.

Those open issues are central to any revised plan.

## V. Adverse arguments and why they remain relevant

The principal arguments against the Court’s result appear in the dissent and in plan proponents’ submissions. They are advocacy and dissenting views, not the governing holding, but they remain relevant to negotiations and any legislative strategy.

**Plan proponents argued:**

- Section 1123(b)(6) is broad and permits any plan provision not expressly forbidden and deemed “appropriate.” Resp. Br. 3–4, 11–14.
- Nonconsensual third-party releases serve the public interest in mass-tort bankruptcies by centralizing claims and preventing a value-destructive race to the courthouse. Resp. Br. 11–14.
- The release was narrow; most creditors with direct claims had consented; and objectors had not identified valuable nonconsensual direct claims. Resp. Br. 11–12.
- The Second Circuit’s test required at least 75% support among voting creditors as a “bare minimum” guardrail. Resp. Br. 14.
- The bankruptcy court found that absent the plan, objectors’ aggregate net recovery would be materially less. Resp. Br. 13.
- The plan offered nonmonetary benefits, including abatement funding, a document repository, transfer to a NewCo, and the Sacklers’ exit from the opioid business. Resp. Br. 13–14.

**The dissent argued:**

- The decision is “wrong on the law and devastating” for opioid victims and families. Op. 227.
- Bankruptcy courts have long approved nondebtor releases in mass-tort cases, and the Code’s “appropriate” standard supports that practice. Op. 227–230.
- Without the release, there would be no $5.5 to $6 billion settlement payment and “no viable path to any victim recovery.” Op. 230.
- Fifty state Attorneys General and many victims supported the plan. Op. 228–230.

The majority rejected these as policy arguments for Congress, not grounds for finding statutory authority. Op. 224–226. The practical warnings in the dissent—lost contribution, litigation race, reduced recoveries—remain the central negotiating risk for creditors if the release is removed.

## VI. Choices for a revised plan

### 1. Consent-based third-party release

The most direct path is to replace the nonconsensual release with a consensual one. The Court left consensual releases open. Op. 226. That could take the form of affirmative opt-in releases, individual settlements, or settlements with States, Tribes, municipalities, and other claimant groups in exchange for consideration. A revised plan could condition the Sacklers’ contribution on obtaining a sufficient quantum of actual consents.

The key unresolved question is what “consent” requires. The Court did not define it. Op. 226. Creditors and plan proponents would need to address whether consent must be affirmative and individualized, how future claimants are treated, whether representative entities can consent for their constituents, and whether silence or failure to opt out can ever suffice. The Second Circuit’s 75% voting guardrail was a feature of plan proponents’ advocacy, Resp. Br. 14, not a rule the Supreme Court adopted.

### 2. Omit the third-party release and preserve direct claims

A revised plan could resolve only claims belonging to the debtor or estate and leave direct claims against the Sacklers outstanding. Section 1123(b)(3) permits a plan to provide for settlement or adjustment of claims belonging to the debtor or estate, including fraudulent-transfer claims. Op. 216–219. The estate’s fraudulent-transfer claims are estate property; direct opioid claims against the Sacklers belong to the victims. Op. 219–221.

Under this option, Purdue could reorganize and resolve estate claims, while individual victims, States, Tribes, and other creditors preserve or pursue their own claims against the Sacklers. The risk is that the Sacklers may decline to contribute, or may contribute less, without a release. The U.S. Trustee argued that exposure to litigation might instead induce the Sacklers to negotiate a better consensual deal; the Court noted that during the appeal the Sacklers had already increased their offer by more than $1 billion to obtain the objecting States’ consent. Op. 225.

### 3. Negotiate enhanced consideration for actual consents

The Court’s decision increases the Sacklers’ litigation exposure and may strengthen creditors’ negotiating leverage. The Trustee predicted that reversal could lead the Sacklers to negotiate consensual releases on more favorable terms. Op. 225. A revised plan could offer enhanced payments or other consideration in exchange for affirmative releases from individual victims, States, Tribes, and other claimant groups.

This option still requires resolving the undefined “consent” issue and the treatment of future claimants. It also requires a funder—likely the Sacklers—willing to pay for the additional consents.

### 4. Explore a full-satisfaction structure

The Court expressly did not decide whether a plan may provide for the full satisfaction of claims against a third-party nondebtor. Op. 226. That leaves room to explore a structure that pays or otherwise fully satisfies the released claims rather than purporting to extinguish them without consent. The excerpts do not describe such a structure or address its feasibility. Any such approach would need to be tested against the Court’s reasoning and the available assets.

### 5. Legislative authorization

The Court stated that if a policy decision is to be made about nonconsensual third-party releases, it is for Congress. Op. 224–226. It also noted that § 524(g) creates a third-party injunction mechanism only for asbestos-related bankruptcies. Op. 220–221. A legislative fix is therefore an external option, not a plan revision, and would require congressional action.

### 6. Effective-plan issues are not presented here

Because the Purdue plan was stayed, the Court did not address whether its reading would justify unwinding plans already effective and substantially consummated. Op. 226. That issue may matter in other cases, but it does not resolve the remand here.

---

## VII. Unresolved facts and immediate considerations

The supplied excerpts do not resolve:

- Whether the Sacklers will contribute without a nonconsensual release, and on what amount and timing.
- Whether sufficient affirmative consents can be obtained from individual victims, future claimants, States, Tribes, Canadian creditors, and other groups.
- What form of consent the courts will accept.
- Whether a full-satisfaction structure is feasible or authorized.
- The exact universe and value of direct claims against the Sacklers; plan proponents asserted the nonconsensual release was narrow and objectors identified no valuable claims, Resp. Br. 11–12, but that was contested advocacy, not a final determination in the excerpts.
- How the remand will proceed and whether a modified plan can be confirmed.

For creditors, the immediate practical points are: the nonconsensual Sackler release cannot be relied on as currently structured; direct claims against the Sacklers should be preserved and evaluated; any consent to a revised release should be documented and given only with a clear understanding of its scope; and the economic terms of any revised plan will depend heavily on whether the Sacklers will pay for actual consent rather than for a nonconsensual release.

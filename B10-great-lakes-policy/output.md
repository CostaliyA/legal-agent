**PRIVILEGED & CONFIDENTIAL — ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT**

**Matter:** *Great Lakes Ins. SE v. Raiders Retreat Realty Co., LLC*, No. 22-500 (U.S. Feb. 21, 2024)

**Client:** Raiders Retreat Realty Co., LLC

---

## I. Executive summary

The Supreme Court resolved **only the enforceability of the policy’s New York choice-of-law clause** in this maritime insurance dispute. It held that choice-of-law provisions in maritime contracts are **presumptively enforceable as a matter of federal maritime law**, subject to narrow exceptions, and that no exception applies here. It therefore enforced the clause selecting New York law and reversed the Third Circuit’s contrary public-policy approach. *Great Lakes Ins. SE v. Raiders Retreat Realty Co.*, slip op. at 1–2, 10–13 (great-lakes-insurance-opinion.pdf).

The Court did **not** decide whether Raiders breached the fire-suppression warranty, whether any breach voids the policy in whole or in part, whether the fire-suppression system caused or contributed to the grounding, whether any exclusion applies, or the merits of Great Lakes’ coverage denial. Those issues remain open. The opinion’s factual recitals and the parties’ briefs are not substitute findings on those points.

**Bottom line:** Great Lakes won the choice-of-law issue; New York law governs the parties’ dispute. Great Lakes did not obtain a merits ruling that Raiders breached the warranty or that coverage is barred. Raiders should now develop the coverage record under New York law and preserve causation, materiality, waiver/estoppel, exclusion, and procedural objections.

## II. What the Supreme Court resolved

### A. The choice-of-law holding

1. **Federal maritime law governs the enforceability of choice-of-law clauses in maritime contracts.** The Court held that such clauses are “presumptively enforceable” as a matter of federal maritime law. *Opinion* at 4–6, 10 (great-lakes-insurance-opinion.pdf). It relied on longstanding precedent and courts of appeals decisions. *Id.* at 4–6.

2. **The presumption applies to marine insurance contracts.** The Court rejected the argument that marine insurance should receive special treatment: “Nothing in *Wilburn Boat* purports to override parties’ choice-of-law clauses in maritime contracts generally, or in the subset of marine insurance contracts specifically.” *Id.* at 9.

3. **The narrow exceptions are limited.** The parties agreed that courts may disregard such clauses when the chosen law would contravene a controlling federal statute or conflict with an established federal maritime policy, or where the parties can furnish no reasonable basis for the chosen jurisdiction. *Id.* at 10–11. The Court applied those exceptions with “substantial deference to the contracting parties.” *Id.* at 11.

4. **No exception applies here.** Raiders did not assert that a federal statute or established federal maritime policy precluded enforcement, and did not claim that choosing New York’s “well-known and highly elaborated commercial law” was unreasonable. *Id.* at 11. The Court therefore enforced the clause. *Id.* at 13.

5. **The Court rejected Raiders’ proposed public-policy exception.** Raiders argued that the clause should yield to the fundamental public policy of the State with the greatest interest in the dispute—here, Pennsylvania. The Court disagreed, declined to adopt that exception, and also declined to adopt Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §187(2)(b) for federal maritime law. *Id.* at 11–13.

6. **The Third Circuit was reversed.** The Third Circuit had held that the clause was presumptively enforceable but must yield to a strong public policy of the State in which suit is brought—Pennsylvania—and had remanded for consideration of Pennsylvania public policy. *Id.* at 2. The Supreme Court reversed that judgment. *Id.* at 13.

### B. What the holding does and does not do

- **It resolves:** The New York choice-of-law clause is enforceable in this maritime insurance dispute. New York law, not Pennsylvania law, supplies the governing law for the parties’ dispute, subject to the narrow exceptions the Court recognized (none of which applied here). *Id.* at 1–2, 10–13.

- **It does not resolve:** The merits of Great Lakes’ denial. The Court did not interpret the policy’s warranty, decide whether Raiders breached it, decide whether any breach voids coverage, decide causation, or apply New York law to the facts. *Id.* at 13.

- **It does not overrule *Wilburn Boat*.** The Court distinguished *Wilburn Boat Co. v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co.*, 348 U.S. 310 (1955), on the ground that *Wilburn Boat* addressed the substantive warranty rule, not the enforceability of a choice-of-law clause. The Court expressly said it “need not resolve any such tension” between *Wilburn Boat* and modern maritime law. *Id.* at 7–8. Justice Thomas’s concurrence criticizes *Wilburn Boat* and would limit it, but that is a concurrence, not the majority holding. *Id.* at 1–6 (Thomas, J., concurring).

- **It does not decide whether the policy is a maritime contract.** The opinion treats the policy as a maritime contract; the excerpts do not show a party challenge to that characterization or a holding on it. *Id.* at 1.

## III. Coverage facts and legal issues that remain open

### A. Warranty breach

**Open.** The opinion recites that Great Lakes asserted Raiders breached the insurance contract by failing to maintain the boat’s fire-suppression system, and that Great Lakes claimed the breach voided the policy “in its entirety.” *Opinion* at 1–2. That is Great Lakes’ assertion, not a court finding.

The petitioner’s brief describes the renewed policy as containing an express warranty that the yacht’s fire-extinguishing equipment was “properly installed” and “maintained in good working order,” including “the weighing of tanks once a year, certification/tagging and recharging as necessary.” Pet. Br. 8 (great-lakes-petitioner-brief.pdf). But the brief description is not a judicial finding, and the excerpts do not reproduce the full warranty, endorsements, definitions, or conditions.

**Unresolved questions:**

- What is the exact warranty language, and is it an express warranty, condition precedent, or covenant?
- Did Raiders actually fail to comply—e.g., fail to weigh tanks annually, certify/tag, or recharge as required?
- If there was noncompliance, was it material?
- Does New York law enforce the warranty strictly, or does it require causation, materiality, or prejudice?
- Does any breach void the entire policy or only coverage for a particular loss?
- Did Great Lakes waive or estop itself from asserting breach by renewing the policy after Raiders’ survey-compliance certification? The petitioner’s brief asserts the survey and certification facts, but no court finding appears in the excerpts. Pet. Br. 8.

### B. Causation and contribution

**Open.** The opinion’s background recites that the fire-suppression system “did not contribute to the accident.” *Opinion* at 1–2. The petitioner’s brief further asserts that “[n]o fire occurred and the fire equipment was not used.” Pet. Br. 8. Those statements are not a holding on causation, and the excerpts do not show the evidentiary basis for them.

**Unresolved questions:**

- Did any fire-suppression deficiency cause or contribute to the grounding or the damage?
- Does the policy or governing New York law require a causal connection between the breached warranty and the loss?
- If the policy contains a strict-compliance warranty, does causation matter?
- What evidence establishes the condition of the fire-suppression system before and after the grounding?

### C. Exclusions and other coverage defenses

**Open.** The excerpts identify only Great Lakes’ breach-of-warranty theory. They do not describe the policy’s exclusions or other coverage defenses, and the Supreme Court did not address any exclusion. *Opinion* at 1–2, 13.

**Unresolved questions:**

- What exclusions, conditions, and limitations appear in the policy?
- Did Great Lakes deny coverage on any ground other than breach of the fire-suppression warranty?
- Are there issues of seaworthiness, negligence, wear and tear, or improper navigation?
- Did Raiders comply with notice, cooperation, and suit-limitation provisions?
- Are there additional insureds, loss-payee issues, or other contractual defenses?

### D. New York law and the substantive coverage standard

**Open.** The choice-of-law holding means New York law governs the parties’ dispute, but the excerpts do not state what New York law provides on warranty breach, materiality, causation, exclusions, waiver, or estoppel. The Court did not apply New York law; it only enforced the clause. *Opinion* at 13.

**Unresolved questions:**

- Under New York law, what is the effect of a breach of an express marine-insurance warranty?
- Does New York require strict compliance or a causal connection?
- Does New York law recognize waiver or estoppel based on renewal after a survey/compliance certification?
- Does any federal maritime rule independently govern the warranty issue, and if so, how does it interact with the New York choice-of-law clause? The majority did not resolve that tension. *Id.* at 7–8.

### E. Procedural and remand questions

**Open.** The excerpts establish the following procedural history: Great Lakes sued for declaratory relief in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania; the District Court enforced the choice-of-law clause and rejected Raiders’ Pennsylvania-law contract claims; the Third Circuit vacated and remanded for consideration of Pennsylvania public policy; the Supreme Court reversed the Third Circuit. *Opinion* at 1–2, 13.

The excerpts do not state the mandate, remand instructions, or whether the District Court judgment is reinstated. They also do not resolve any venue or forum clause.

**Unresolved questions:**

- What is the current procedural posture after the Supreme Court’s reversal—reinstatement of the District Court judgment, remand for New York-law coverage proceedings, or something else?
- Can Raiders amend or assert New York-law defenses and counterclaims?
- Does any forum/venue clause affect where the coverage dispute proceeds? The petitioner’s brief references a clause tied to the federal district where Raiders or its insurance agent resides, but the excerpt is truncated and the Supreme Court did not address it. Pet. Br. 8.
- What is the deadline for further proceedings, and what record will the District Court consider?

2. **Obtain the claim file and denial letter** to confirm every ground on which Great Lakes denied coverage.

5. **Develop causation evidence** on the grounding and damage, including surveyor and expert reports.
6. **Research New York law** on express marine-insurance warranties, materiality, causation, waiver/estoppel, and exclusions.
7. **Confirm the procedural posture and mandate** after the Supreme Court’s reversal, including whether and how the District Court will apply New York law.
